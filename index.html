Investigate and Learn · Reading

Intramolecular vs. intermolecular forces

When atoms bond to form a compound, the attractions that hold those atoms together are called intramolecular forces. The attractions between separate particles are called intermolecular forces (IMFs).

IMFs are much weaker than the bonds inside molecules. You can see the difference when you boil a liquid: boiling usually pulls molecules apart, but it does not break the bonds inside them.

Sort it: inside or between?

Why IMFs matter

IMFs affect every molecular substance. The strength of the IMFs changes boiling and melting points, how sticky and cohesive a substance is, how easily it evaporates (vapor pressure), and how well it dissolves.

Careful with generalizations: You may read that substances with the strongest IMFs are solids at room temperature. That is only a rough pattern for molecular substances. Ionic and metallic solids are held together by bonds, not by IMFs, so the pattern does not explain them.

Investigate and Learn · The four types

Types of IMFs

There are four IMFs. They are listed below from weakest to strongest. Tap each card to read more.

London dispersion forces and size

Every substance has London dispersion forces. Electrons are always moving, so for a moment one side of a molecule can have more electrons than the other. That brief imbalance creates a temporary dipole, and it can attract a neighboring molecule. Molecules with more electrons have stronger dispersion forces, so they need more energy to boil.

Dipole-dipole forces: CO vs. N2

Dipole-dipole forces happen between polar molecules. The slightly positive side of one molecule attracts the slightly negative side of another. Both CO and N2 have 14 electrons, so their dispersion forces are similar. Tap the button to see which molecule is polar.

CO has a higher boiling point because it is polar. It has dipole-dipole forces on top of its dispersion forces. N2 is nonpolar and has only dispersion forces.

Hydrogen bonding

Hydrogen bonding is a special, strong type of dipole-dipole attraction. It happens when a hydrogen atom is bonded to a very electronegative atom (N, O, or F) and is attracted to an N, O, or F atom on a nearby molecule. Water (H2O), methanol (CH3OH), and hydrogen fluoride (HF) all show hydrogen bonding.

Misconception: The name "hydrogen bond" makes it sound like a chemical bond. It is not. It is an intermolecular force between two molecules.

Ion-dipole forces

When an ionic compound dissolves in water, the water molecules surround the ions. The attraction between an ion and a polar molecule is an ion-dipole force. It is the strongest IMF in this lesson.

Investigate and Learn · Metallic bonding

The sea of electrons model

Metals have lower ionization energies than nonmetals, so their atoms let go of valence electrons relatively easily. Metal atoms also have several empty valence orbitals. Neighboring atoms' empty orbitals overlap, so the electrons can move freely from one atom to the next.

The sea of electrons model says that these electrons form a "sea" of delocalized electrons. The atoms become positive cores arranged in a lattice, and the electrons move between them. Metallic bonding is the attraction between those positive cores and the delocalized electrons.

Watch out: Metal atoms become positive cores, but their electrons do not stay with one atom. The electrons belong to the whole metal. The total number of protons and electrons does not change.

Reference

Key words

Intramolecular forceA force that holds atoms together inside a compound.
Intermolecular force (IMF)An attraction between neighboring particles.
London dispersion forceThe weakest IMF. It comes from temporary dipoles caused by moving electrons.
DipoleA part of a molecule where the charge is uneven, so one side is slightly positive and the other slightly negative.
Dipole-dipole forceAn attraction between the slightly positive end of one polar molecule and the slightly negative end of another.
Hydrogen bondingA strong IMF between a hydrogen atom bonded to N, O, or F and an N, O, or F atom on another molecule.
Ion-dipole forceThe strongest IMF in this lesson. It happens between an ion and a polar molecule, such as water.
Delocalized electronsElectrons that move freely among many atoms instead of belonging to one atom.
Metallic bondThe attraction between metal cores and delocalized electrons.
Sea of electrons modelThe model that explains metallic bonding with delocalized electrons moving between metal atoms.
ConductivityHow well a substance transfers heat or electricity.
MalleabilityThe ability to be hammered or pressed into a new shape without breaking.
DuctilityThe ability to be drawn into a thin wire without breaking.
